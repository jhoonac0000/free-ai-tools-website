How to Write a Resume That Gets Interviews

A few years ago I helped a friend rewrite his resume. He was genuinely good at his job — the kind of person colleagues went to when things broke — yet he'd sent out around forty applications and heard back from nobody. Not one interview. When I opened his resume, I understood why in about six seconds. And that's the whole problem, because six seconds is roughly all a busy hiring manager gives you too.

His experience was buried under a long "objective" paragraph, his best results were on page two, and every bullet sounded like it was copied from a job description. The man hadn't changed. The page had. We spent one evening fixing the page, and the same person started getting replies within two weeks. This guide is that evening, written down: the rules we used, the mistakes we removed, and the order we fixed them in.

1. The six-second scan is real

Nobody reads a resume like a book. A hiring manager scans it — current role first, then the role before that, then education, then any reason to keep going. If those don't line up with the job almost straight away, the rest of the page rarely gets a look. Eye-tracking studies of recruiters have shown this for years, and honestly, you don't need a study. Imagine it's 4:45 pm, you have 90 applications to get through, and dinner is waiting. You would scan too.

So design for the scan before you design for the read. That means clear section headings a tired eye can land on, generous spacing, and your most relevant role at the top — not buried in strict reverse order if an older role matches the job better. One page is the target for most people; two is acceptable once you have a decade of genuinely relevant experience. Anything longer is usually a sign that editing hasn't happened yet.

A quick test I now use on every resume: hand it to someone for exactly six seconds, take it back, and ask what job the person is suited for. If they can't answer, the top of the page isn't doing its job, no matter how good page two is.

2. Start with the job ad, not your history

Most people write a resume by listing everything they've ever done and then shopping it around. It feels efficient. It rarely works. The better starting point is the specific job ad in front of you, because that ad is a cheat sheet: it tells you exactly which skills, tools and words the employer cares about, in their own language.

That language matters more than it used to. Many companies filter applications with software before a human ever sees them, and the filter looks for the ad's own keywords. If the ad says "inventory management" and your resume says "stock handling", the filter may never connect the two — even though a human instantly would. Going through testing this myself, the fix is simple and honest: use the ad's exact words for skills you genuinely have. Never claim a skill you don't have; an interview will expose that within minutes, and it's a miserable five minutes for everyone.

A five-minute keyword pass

  • Print or copy the job ad, and highlight every skill, tool and qualification it names.
  • Go through your resume and mark where each one appears — in the employer's words, not yours.
  • For anything true that's missing, add it where it honestly belongs: a bullet, a skills line, a course.
  • For anything you don't have, leave it out. One gap is survivable; one lie isn't.

That's the whole trick. Twenty honest minutes of matching beats forty generic applications, every single time.

3. Numbers beat adjectives

Here's the fastest upgrade you can make to almost any bullet point. "Responsible for customer service" could describe anyone who's ever stood behind a counter. "Handled 40 customers a day with a 4.8 satisfaction rating" describes one specific person — you. Adjectives are claims. Numbers are evidence, and readers trust evidence.

Go through every bullet on your resume and ask one question: can I add a number, a timeframe, or a result? How many people, how much money, how often, how much faster, how many fewer errors. You don't need to have been a manager to have numbers. A retail assistant knows roughly how many customers a shift brings. A junior developer knows how many tickets they close a week. Estimates you're comfortable defending in an interview are fine; invented precision is not.

Before and after, from real rewrites

  • "Helped with social media" → "Posted 3 times a week; grew the account from 800 to 2,300 followers in six months."
  • "Good with customers" → "Served around 60 customers a day; named employee of the month twice."
  • "Worked on reports" → "Built the weekly sales report used by a team of 12; cut its prep time from 2 hours to 30 minutes."

Notice none of these people changed jobs. They just described the same work like someone who understood its value. If a bullet survives the number question twice with "no, there's nothing measurable here", that bullet is probably filler — and filler is what pushes your best material onto page two.

4. One page, tailored every time

A generic resume sent everywhere is, in my experience, the slowest possible way to get hired. It feels productive — fifty applications in an afternoon! — but each one lands as "almost right" and almost-right goes in the same pile as wrong. Employers don't hire your whole history. They hire the slice of it that matches their problem.

The system that works is boring and effective. Keep one master resume with everything in it: every role, every bullet, every course, every number you can remember. It can be four pages; nobody sees it but you. Then, for each application, copy it and delete your way down to one page, keeping only what speaks to that ad. Deleting is fast. Deciding what survives is the actual skill, and it gets easier every time you do it.

My twenty-minute tailoring routine

  1. Read the ad twice; highlight the keywords (section 2 above).
  2. Copy the master resume; delete any role older than ten years unless it's directly relevant.
  3. Reorder bullets inside each role so the matching ones sit on top.
  4. Cut every bullet that doesn't support this application, however proud of it you are.
  5. Check the six-second test: right job obvious at a glance? Then save as PDF and send.

Twenty minutes per application sounds slow until you compare reply rates. Based on what I've seen helping people with this, tailored applications regularly double the responses of spray-and-pray — and they take fewer total hours, because you're sending ten good ones instead of fifty hopeful ones.

5. The cover letter: three paragraphs, not an essay

People dread cover letters because they imagine a formal essay. Scrap that image. A working cover letter is three short paragraphs, and each has exactly one job. Anything longer is rarely read, and anything that opens with "I am writing to express my interest in the position of..." gets skipped on sight — it says nothing, in the most words possible.

Paragraph one: why this company, specifically. Name something real — a product you use, a project you noticed, a market they're entering. Two or three sentences that couldn't be pasted into another company's letter. Paragraph two: your single best piece of proof. Not your whole career; the one result that best matches their problem, with its number attached. Paragraph three: a polite close that suggests a next step — you'd welcome a short call, you're available for an interview this week, something human and low-pressure.

One honest warning from experience: a tailored three-paragraph letter beats a generic full page, but no letter at all beats a copy-pasted one with the wrong company name in it. That mistake happens constantly, and it's an instant rejection. If you reuse a letter — and you will — read the first paragraph out loud before sending. Every time.

6. Mistakes that quietly end applications

Most rejected resumes aren't rejected for one big flaw. They die from small, avoidable ones. These are the repeat offenders I see again and again:

  • A typo in the first three lines. Fair or not, it reads as carelessness about the job itself. Read the top of the page twice; that's where eyes land.
  • A photo, when none is expected. In many countries and industries a photo adds bias risk and zero information. If the ad or local custom asks for one, include it; otherwise, leave it out.
  • Unexplained gaps with no line at all. Silence invites the reader to imagine the worst. One honest line — "Family care, 2023–2024" — beats a mysterious blank every time.
  • An email address from another era. Anything jokey, dated or hard to spell undermines an otherwise professional page. Your name in some clean form costs nothing.
  • Lying about a role, a date or a degree. Background checks are routine now, and "embellished" is just lying with better PR. If it's found — and it often is — the offer disappears.
  • Sending a format the machine can't read. Heavily designed templates with text inside images can defeat application software entirely. Pretty is good; parseable is essential. A clean PDF from a simple layout is the safe default.

None of these take talent to fix. They take one careful proofread by someone who isn't you — a friend, ideally, because your own eyes stopped seeing your typos three drafts ago.

7. How this skill earns you money

Two ways. The direct one first: a better resume is the cheapest raise you will ever get, because every pay rise in a salaried life starts with an interview, and every interview starts with this page. Even a single extra offer changes your negotiating position completely — it's much easier to ask for more when walking away is a real option.

The second way: people happily pay for resume help, especially before a career change, a promotion push or a move abroad. Sellers typically bundle a resume rewrite with a cover letter and a LinkedIn tidy-up, priced as one package. After testing this kind of offer myself, the pattern is clear — one happy client refers friends, because everyone knows someone quietly job-hunting. You don't need credentials to start; you need two or three before-and-after samples (with permission) and the honesty to only sell what you can actually improve.

Conclusion: the page is not you — but it's the door

Here's what I want you to take from all this: a rejected resume is usually not a rejected person. My friend wasn't unemployable; his page was unreadable in the only six seconds that mattered. Your experience is probably better than your resume makes it look. Fix the scan, borrow the employer's own words, put numbers where adjectives were, tailor ruthlessly, and proofread like an enemy. Then send fewer, better applications — and let the page finally argue as well as you would in person.

Frequently asked questions

How long should my resume actually be?

For most people, one page. It's not a moral rule, it's a reading rule: the person scanning has limited time and a tall pile, so a single focused page gets fully read while a long one gets sampled. If you have ten-plus years of directly relevant experience, two pages is fine. Beyond that, you're almost certainly keeping material that should have been cut — and the cutting is what makes the remainder stronger.

Should I include a photo on my resume?

It depends on where you're applying, and the local custom matters more than any global rule. In much of the US, UK and Canada, photos are discouraged and can even create bias problems for the employer. In parts of Europe, the Middle East and Asia, a professional photo is commonly expected. When in doubt, follow the job ad: if it doesn't ask, and your target market doesn't expect one, leave it out and let the results speak.

What if I have a gap in my work history?

Explain it in one honest line and move on. "Family care, 2023–2024", "Full-time study, 2022", "Freelance projects, 2021–2023" — any of these is better than a silent hole, because silence lets the reader invent a worse story than the truth. If you did anything during the gap — courses, volunteering, freelance bits, caring responsibilities — name it. Gaps are normal; unexplained gaps are what make recruiters nervous.

Can I use the same resume for every job?

You can, and it's the most common reason good people get ignored. Employers hire the slice of your history that matches their problem, and a generic resume makes them do the matching work themselves — which, at six seconds per resume, they won't. Keep a master resume with everything, then spend twenty minutes cutting a tailored one-pager per application. Fewer applications, far more replies: that's the trade, and it's worth it.

Is it worth paying someone to write my resume?

Sometimes. If you've been applying for months with a page you know is weak, a good rewrite can cost less than one extra week of unemployment. But check the basics first: many paid "rewrites" are just prettier templates with the same vague bullets. A good service asks about your results and numbers, shows before-and-after samples, and tailors to a specific role.
