How to Write a Resume That Gets Interviews

Most resumes don't get rejected because the person can't do the job. They get rejected because nobody could see the proof in the first six seconds. This guide is about fixing that.

1. The six-second scan is real

Nobody reads a resume like a book. A hiring manager scans it — current role first, then the role before that, then education, then any reason to keep going. If those don't line up with the job almost straight away, the rest of the page rarely gets a look. Design for the scan first: clear headings, one page if you can, and the most relevant role at the top — not the oldest.

2. Numbers beat adjectives

"Responsible for customer service" could describe anyone. "Handled 40 customers a day, with a 4.8 satisfaction rating" describes you. Go through every bullet and ask: can I add a number, a time, or a result? If the answer is no twice in a row, that bullet is probably filler.

3. Match the ad's words — honestly

Many companies filter applications by keywords before a human ever sees them. If the ad says "inventory management" and you write "stock handling", the filter may miss you. Use the ad's exact words for skills you genuinely have. Never claim a skill you do not have; interviews expose that in minutes.

4. One page, tailored every time

A generic resume sent everywhere is the slowest way to get hired. Keep one master resume with everything, then delete lines to build a one-page version for each role. Tailoring takes twenty minutes and regularly doubles the reply rate, because the reader sees their job in your resume — not just your history.

5. The cover letter is three paragraphs, not an essay

Keep it to three short paragraphs. First: why this company, specifically. Second: your single best piece of proof. Third: a polite close that suggests a next step. Anything longer rarely gets read — and anything that opens with "I am writing to express my interest..." gets skipped on sight.

6. Mistakes that end applications

  • A typo in the first three lines — it reads as carelessness about the job itself.
  • A photo, unless the job or country expects one — it adds bias risk and no information.
  • Unexplained gaps with no line at all — one short line ("Family care, 2023–2024") beats silence.
  • Lying about a role, a date or a degree — background checks are routine now.

7. How this skill earns

People happily pay for resume help, especially before a career change or a move abroad. Sellers offer resume rewrites, LinkedIn makeovers and cover letters as a bundle, and one happy client often refers friends. The skill also pays you directly: a better resume is the cheapest raise you will ever get, because every interview starts with it.
