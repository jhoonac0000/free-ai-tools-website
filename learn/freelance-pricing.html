How to Price Your Freelance Work (Without Guessing)

Most new freelancers pick a rate by copying someone else's. That is how you end up busy, tired and broke. Here is the honest way to price.

1. Start from the life you need, not the market's noise

Write down the annual income you actually need to live — rent, food, transport, family, a small buffer. That number is the foundation. Market rates are useful information, but they describe other people's costs, not yours. A rate that does not cover your life is not competitive; it is a slow exit from freelancing.

2. Count billable hours, not working hours

A 40-hour week is almost never 40 paid hours. Pitching, admin, learning, invoicing and waiting are unpaid. Most freelancers honestly bill 20–25 hours a week. Use 1,000 hours a year as a starting point. If you divide your income target by 2,000 hours, you have invented a rate that assumes you never stop billing — and you will feel underpaid within months.

3. Add expenses, then tax — in that order

Software, internet, equipment, courses and a phone are real costs. Add them to your income target first. Then gross the total up for tax: if your tax rate is 20%, you need to earn $1.25 for every $1 you keep. The calculator on this site does that maths for you, but the order matters more than the tool: expenses first, tax second, rate last.

4. The result is a floor, not a price

Your calculated rate is the minimum you can accept without losing money. Real quotes go above it: urgent work costs more, specialist work costs more, and clients who pay late cost more (because waiting is a cost too). Never apologize for a rate that is simply honest. Clients who want the cheapest option are rarely the clients who pay on time.

5. Quote projects, not hours, when you can

Clients buy outcomes, not hours. Take your hourly floor, multiply by an honest hour estimate, add a buffer for revisions, and quote one project number. Project pricing also protects you: as you get faster, your effective hourly rate rises instead of falling.

6. Re-price twice a year

Expenses rise, tax rules change, and your skills improve. A rate from two years ago is almost certainly too low. Put two dates in your calendar — January and July — and re-run the numbers. Raising your rate for new clients is normal business; it is not disloyalty to old ones.

7. How this skill earns

Pricing help is itself a service: new freelancers pay for rate reviews, quote templates and pricing coaching. And every freelancer who prices honestly becomes a better client for other services — web work, video work, writing — because they understand what good work costs.
