How to Price Your Freelance Work (Without Guessing)

The first freelance rate I ever quoted came straight out of thin air. A client asked what I charged, I panicked, named the hourly figure a friend of mine charged — he did different work, in a different city, with different bills — and won the job. Two months later I was working evenings to cover rent, doing good work for a rate that quietly lost me money on every single hour. If that story sounds familiar, this guide is the maths I wish someone had handed me before I quoted anyone anything.

Here's the honest truth nobody tells new freelancers: your rate is not a personality trait, and it isn't a measure of your worth as a human. It's arithmetic. There is a number below which you lose money, whether you feel it immediately or not — and once you can calculate that number calmly, quoting stops being an emotional event and becomes a business decision.

1. Start from the life you need, not the market's noise

Write down the annual income you actually need to live — rent, food, transport, family responsibilities, healthcare, and a small buffer for the months that go sideways. Not the income you'd like. The one below which life stops working. That number is the foundation of your rate, and nobody can tell you what it is except your own bank statements.

Market rates are useful information, but they describe other people's costs, not yours. The freelancer charging half your rate might live rent-free with family; the one charging double might be funding a mortgage and two kids. Copying either of them is how you end up busy, tired and still broke. A rate that doesn't cover your life isn't "competitive". It's a slow, polite exit from freelancing — you just don't feel it until month three.

One practical tip before we go further: be honest about the buffer. New freelancers always skip it, because it makes the number bigger and scarier. But income arrives in lumps, clients pay late, and laptops die at the worst possible moment. A buffer of even 10% turns those events from crises into inconveniences.

2. Count billable hours, not working hours

A 40-hour week is almost never 40 paid hours. Pitching, admin, learning, invoicing, waiting on replies — none of that gets billed. Add holidays, sick days and the quiet weeks between projects, and most freelancers honestly bill somewhere around 20–25 hours a week. That's not laziness; that's the shape of the work.

A sane starting point is 1,000 billable hours a year. Here's why the number matters so much: if you divide your income target by 2,000 hours (a full-time employee's year), you've invented a rate that assumes you never stop billing — no admin, no holidays, no dry spells. You'll feel mysteriously underpaid within months and blame your clients, when the real culprit was the denominator. Use 1,000 hours, and the same income suddenly produces a rate that can actually survive contact with real life.

A quick reality check on your own week

  • Track one normal week and mark which hours a client actually paid for. Most people are surprised; that's the point.
  • Count the recurring unpaid blocks: proposals, calls, revisions admin, bookkeeping, marketing yourself.
  • Whatever's left is your real billable week. Use it, not the fantasy version, in every calculation below.

3. Add expenses, then tax — in that order

Freelancing has costs a salary quietly hides. Software subscriptions, internet, equipment, courses, a phone, coworking, transaction fees — individually small, together very real. List yours for a year, honestly, and add the total to your income target before you calculate anything else. If you skip this step, you're donating your expenses to your clients.

Then gross the total up for tax. The logic is simple: if your effective tax rate is 20%, then for every $1 you want to keep, you need to earn $1.25. The exact percentage depends on your country and setup — use your real one, or a cautious estimate if you're new. The order matters more than the precision: expenses first, tax second, rate last. 4. The result is a floor, not a price

Divide your total (income + expenses, grossed up for tax) by your billable hours, and you get the most important number in your freelance life: your floor rate. This is the minimum you can accept without paying to work. It's not your price list. It's the line below which "yes" becomes self-sabotage, however flattering the project sounds.

A worked example, with round numbers

  • You need $30,000 a year to live, and your work expenses are $3,000. Subtotal: $33,000.
  • At a 20% tax rate, gross that up: $33,000 ÷ 0.8 = $41,250 you must actually bill.
  • Divide by 1,000 billable hours: your floor is $41.25 an hour. Quote above it; never below.

Real quotes go above the floor for honest reasons: urgent work costs more, specialist work costs more, and clients who pay in 45 days cost more than clients who pay in 7, because waiting is a cost too. And one emotional rule that took me too long to learn — never apologise for a rate that is simply honest. State it, stop talking, and let the client respond. The freelancers who get in trouble are rarely the expensive ones; they're the ones who negotiated against themselves before the client said a word.

5. Quote projects, not hours, when you can

Clients buy outcomes, not hours. Nobody dreams of purchasing fourteen hours of design; they dream of a finished logo. So when you can, take your hourly floor, multiply it by an honest hour estimate, add a buffer for revisions and the unexpected, and quote one project number. One number is easier for a client to approve than an open-ended meter, and it protects you in a way hourly billing never will: as you get faster, your effective hourly rate rises instead of falling. Hourly billing literally fines you for improving.

Two habits make project quoting safe. First, define the scope in writing — what's included, how many revision rounds, what counts as a new request. Most "difficult client" stories are really "undefined scope" stories. Second, split larger projects into stages with a deposit before work starts: a common pattern is a third upfront, a third at the midpoint, a third on delivery. If a client refuses any deposit, that is information about how the rest of the project will go.

6. Re-price twice a year (and how to tell clients)

Expenses rise, tax rules change, and — easy to forget — you get better and faster, which makes your time worth more, not less. A rate from two years ago is almost certainly too low. Put two dates in your calendar, January and July, and re-run the numbers from section 3. Fifteen minutes, twice a year, protects your income better than any productivity hack.

Raising rates for new clients needs no announcement at all; you simply quote the new number. Existing clients deserve a short, friendly note, and it matters less than you fear. Keep it simple and give notice:

  • "From March, my rate for new projects moves to X — projects we've already agreed stay at the current rate."
  • Give four to six weeks' notice, so nobody feels ambushed mid-project.
  • Don't over-explain or apologise. A plumber doesn't send an essay when prices change; neither should you.

In my experience, good clients barely blink — they assumed your rates would rise eventually, because everyone's do. The clients who leave over a fair increase were usually the ones the increase was designed to protect you from.

7. How this skill earns you money

First, directly: every hour you work above your floor instead of below it is money that compounds across your whole career. The difference between a guessed rate and a calculated one is often the difference between freelancing as a stopgap and freelancing as a business.

Second, pricing help is itself a service. New freelancers pay for rate reviews, quote templates and pricing coaching — especially in the first year, when every quote feels like a guess and a dare. If you can walk someone through the maths in this guide, with their real numbers, you've sold something genuinely valuable. And there's a quiet third effect: freelancers who price honestly become better clients for everyone else's services — web work, video work, writing — because they understand what good work actually costs.

Conclusion: a rate you can defend is a rate you can say calmly

If your current rate came from copying a stranger, forgive yourself — almost everyone's did. But replace it. Work out the life you need, count your honest billable hours, add expenses then tax, and find your floor. Quote projects above it, re-price twice a year, and stop apologising for arithmetic. The goal was never to be expensive. The goal is to still be freelancing, happily, three years from now — and that starts with a number that actually adds up.

Frequently asked questions

How do I work out my minimum hourly rate?

Add up the annual income you need to live, add your yearly work expenses, then gross the total up for tax — at 20% tax, divide by 0.8. Divide that figure by your realistic billable hours, around 1,000 a year for most freelancers. The result is your floor: the lowest rate you can accept without losing money. Real quotes should sit above it. The free rate calculator on this site runs exactly this sequence with your own numbers.

What if clients say my rate is too high?

Some will, and that's information, not failure. A rate built from your real costs can't drop much without you subsidising the client, so the honest options are a smaller scope for a smaller budget, or a polite no. What you shouldn't do is discount on the spot out of discomfort — decide beforehand what you'll flex on (scope, timeline, payment terms) and what you won't (your floor). Clients who value the work usually respect a calm, explained number.

Should I charge hourly or per project?

Per project, whenever the scope can be defined — clients prefer one number, and your effective rate grows as you get faster instead of being capped by the clock. Hourly still makes sense for open-ended work like ongoing support or consulting, where nobody can honestly estimate the total. Either way, both should be built on the same floor rate: estimate the hours, multiply, and sanity-check the project price against it before quoting.

How often should I raise my freelance rates?

Twice a year is a good rhythm — January and July work well. Re-run your numbers each time: expenses creep up, tax situations change, and your speed and skill genuinely increase your value. Apply new rates to new clients immediately, and give existing clients a short note with a few weeks' notice. If you haven't raised rates in two years, you're almost certainly charging less than your own floor by now.

Do I need to charge tax on top of my rate?

That depends on your country's rules and your registration status, and it's one area where a short conversation with a local accountant pays for itself. What matters for this guide is different: whether tax comes out of your rate or is added on top, your calculation must account for it. Grossing up during pricing (section 3) makes sure the money you planned to keep is still there after the tax bill arrives, instead of being a nasty surprise.
