Freelance Rate Calculator

Enter what you want to earn, your real billable hours and your costs — get the minimum rate you must charge. All maths runs in your browser.

Learn: how to use this well

  1. Billable hours are not working hours. A 40-hour week is rarely 40 paid hours. Admin, pitching and learning are unpaid — 20–25 billable hours a week is realistic for most freelancers.
  2. Add expenses before tax, not after. Software, internet, equipment and courses are real costs. A rate that ignores them is a pay cut you gave yourself.
  3. Tax comes off the top. The calculator grosses your rate up for tax so the number you quote already includes it. Never quote the pre-tax number.
  4. The result is a floor, not a price. Charge at least this. Charge more when the work is urgent, specialist, or for a client who can pay it.
  5. Re-run it twice a year. Expenses rise, tax rules change, and your skills improve. A rate from two years ago is almost certainly too low.
  6. Quote projects from the rate. Hourly rate × honest hours = project price. Add a buffer for revisions, then quote one number.

Pro tips

  • If the hourly number shocks you, your billable-hours number is probably too high. Lower it and watch the rate become honest.
  • Keep one "quiet month" in your maths: divide by 11 months of income, not 12.
  • Never compete on being cheapest. Compete on being reliable — it pays for years.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my rate higher than I expected?

Because most people forget expenses, tax and unpaid hours. The calculator counts all three — that is why the number looks higher than a salary divided by 2,000 hours.

How many billable hours should I enter?

Start with 1,000 a year (about 20 a week for 50 weeks). If you consistently bill more, raise it — the rate will fall, honestly.

Should I charge this rate to every client?

Use it as your minimum. Big companies and urgent work can pay more; small favours for friends are your choice, not your rate.

Does this include holidays and sick days?

Yes, indirectly: fewer billable hours in the year already accounts for time off. That is exactly why billable hours matter more than working hours.
